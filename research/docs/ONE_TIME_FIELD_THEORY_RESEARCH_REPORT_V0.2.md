## Abstract

This report consolidates six completed finite-dimensional experiments and a seventh spatially extended numerical experiment developed in the GENESIS 2.4 research layer. The program investigates whether a candidate temporal variable can be related to field activity, whether relational time is merely a reparameterization of an external evolution parameter, and whether an autonomous clock degree of freedom can be coupled to scalar, phase, and tensor-array states through a shared action.

Experiments 1–4 examine activity-derived clock rates, chain-rule reparameterization, phenomenological clock-to-field feedback, and numerical convergence of that feedback. Experiments 5–6 introduce an action-derived dynamical clock and a unified finite-dimensional action with scalar, phase, and tensor-array domains. Experiment 7 extends the action ansatz to a periodic one-dimensional scalar field and evaluates grid-refinement error and energy drift.

The results establish that the chosen equations can be implemented, integrated, and tested with controlled numerical behavior. They do not establish that physical time is generated by these fields, that the proposed coupling exists in nature, or that the framework is a complete unification of known interactions. The report therefore treats One Time Field Theory (OTFT) as a research hypothesis and mathematical construction whose next requirements are covariant formulation, dimensional calibration, independent observable predictions, and empirical confrontation.

## 1. Research question and scope

The central working question is:

> Can a single dynamical framework represent field evolution and temporal structure as mutually related degrees of freedom, while preserving clear mathematical limits and recoverable simpler models?

The phrase “One Time Field” is used here as a guiding hypothesis: temporal structure may be investigated as part of a common dynamical description rather than assumed to be only an external label. This is not a claim that time has already been shown to be a physical field. The experiments separate three distinct ideas:

1. **Evolution parameter:** the independent variable used by a numerical integrator.
2. **Relational clock reading:** a quantity constructed from the state of the system.
3. **Dynamical clock coordinate:** an additional degree of freedom with its own equation of motion.

These concepts are not interchangeable. A change of parameter alone does not create a new physical effect; a feedback law or action coupling is an additional model assumption and must be tested separately.

The research implementation is isolated under `research/` and does not modify the legacy GENESIS C simulator.

## 2. Mathematical foundation

### 2.1 Field-state representation

The finite-dimensional research layer represents scalar states, phase states, and tensor-array states on a shared finite graph. The graph and arrays are computational structures. In particular, the tensor-array state is not yet a geometric tensor field with specified coordinate transformation laws.

### 2.2 Activity-derived candidate clock

For a state Ψ(λ), the research prototype defines domain activity measures from mean-square rates of change:

[
A_phi=\operatorname{mean}_i\left(\frac{d\phi_i}{d\lambda}\right)^2,
\quad
A_\theta=\operatorname{mean}_i\left(\frac{d\theta_i}{d\lambda}\right)^2,
\quad
A_T=\operatorname{mean}_{i,k}\left(\frac{dT_{ik}}{d\lambda}\right)^2.
]

A weighted aggregate is (A=a_phi A_phi+a_	heta A_	heta+a_T A_T), and a candidate clock rate is

[
\frac{d\tau}{d\lambda}=b+\eta A,\qquad b+\eta A>0.
]

This is a constructed observable. Without dimensional calibration and operational measurement, it is not entropy, proper time, or a measured physical clock.

### 2.3 Reparameterization

For a positive state-dependent rate (r(\Psi)),

[
\frac{d\tau}{d\lambda}=r(\Psi),\qquad
\frac{d\Psi}{d\tau}=\frac{F(\Psi)}{r(\Psi)}
]

is a chain-rule reparameterization of (d\Psi/d\lambda=F(\Psi)). When integrated consistently, the state-space trajectory is unchanged; only the parameter used to label progress changes.

### 2.4 Phenomenological feedback

Experiment 3 adds a delayed feedback term to the scalar dynamics, driven by the previous activity-derived clock rate. The gain is an explicit phenomenological parameter. It is not derived from the earlier reparameterization identity. This distinction is essential: the observed trajectory separation follows from adding a new interaction term, not from relabeling time.

### 2.5 Action-derived finite-dimensional clock

Experiments 5–6 use an autonomous clock coordinate (T_c), with positive inertia (I_c), stiffness κ, and coupling (g). A representative finite-dimensional Lagrangian is

[
L=\frac12\dot q^TM\dot q+\frac12 I_c\dot T_c^2-U(q,T_c),
]

[
U(q,T_c)=V(q)+\frac12\kappa T_c^2-gT_c C(q).
]

Here (q) collects the field degrees of freedom, (M) is a constant positive block-diagonal kinetic matrix, (V) is the selected field potential, and (C) is a bounded coupling observable. The equations are

[
M\ddot q=-\nabla V+gT_c\nabla C,
\qquad
I_c\ddot T_c=-\kappa T_c+gC.
]

This is a finite-dimensional mechanical action. (M) is not a spacetime metric, and the construction does not by itself imply Lorentz covariance.

For the unified model, (C=C_s+C_\theta+C_T), with bounded scalar and tensor amplitudes and a graph phase-difference term. The action is invariant under a uniform phase shift because the phase interaction depends only on phase differences. The associated conserved Noether charge is

[
P_\theta=\sum_i p_{\theta_i}.
]

This is the total canonical phase momentum of the finite-dimensional model, not automatically physical angular momentum or a spacetime Noether charge.

### 2.6 Spatially extended scalar-field ansatz

Experiment 7 introduces a real scalar field φ(x,t) on a periodic one-dimensional domain of length (L), coupled to one autonomous clock coordinate (T(t)):

[
S=\int dt\left\{
\int_0^L dx\left[
\frac12(\partial_t\phi)^2
-\frac12c^2(\partial_x\phi)^2
-\frac12m^2\phi^2
\right]
+\frac12 I\dot T^2-\frac12\kappa T^2+gT C[\phi]
\right\},
]

[
C[\phi]=\int_0^L dx\,\frac12\frac{\phi^2}{1+\phi^2}.
]

The resulting equations are

[
\partial_t^2\phi=c^2\partial_x^2\phi-m^2\phi+
gT\frac{\phi}{(1+\phi^2)^2},
\qquad
I\ddot T=-\kappa T+gC[\phi].
]

The conserved autonomous energy is

[
E=\int_0^L dx\left[
\frac12\phi_t^2+\frac12c^2\phi_x^2+\frac12m^2\phi^2
\right]
+\frac12 I\dot T^2+\frac12\kappa T^2-gTC[\phi].
]

The numerical implementation uses a periodic centered second-difference Laplacian and velocity-Verlet time integration. The timestep scales with the grid spacing to control the wave-equation Courant condition. The grid-refinement study compares (N=32,64,128) to an (N=256) reference solution at a common final parameter time.

## 3. Experimental program and findings

| Experiment | Procedure | Result | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Field-derived temporal activity | Compute mean-square scalar, phase, and tensor-array rates and combine them into a candidate clock rate | A reproducible state-derived clock observable can be computed | It is not a measured proper-time law or entropy |
| 2. External versus relational time | Integrate the same dynamics using a positive state-dependent chain-rule reparameterization | State-space paths agree to numerical tolerance when the chain rule is implemented consistently | Reparameterization alone creates no new physical dynamics |
| 3. Explicit clock-to-field feedback | Add a one-step-delayed activity-dependent source to scalar evolution | Nonzero feedback gain separates trajectories; zero gain recovers the control | The feedback law is a postulate, not a derived physical interaction |
| 4. Feedback convergence and strength sweep | Refine timestep and vary feedback gain | At final parameter time 5, the state separation approaches about 0.10406; the gain sweep is monotonic in the tested range | The distance mixes variables with different units and is not yet a calibrated physical observable |
| 5. Action-derived clock coupling | Couple a dynamical clock coordinate to a bounded scalar observable through an augmented potential | The coupled trajectory differs from the control while its own Hamiltonian is conserved to small integration error | This does not prove that physical time feeds back into matter |
| 6. Unified shared action | Couple scalar, phase, tensor-array, and clock degrees of freedom under one finite-dimensional action | Shared action, controlled zero-coupling limit, global phase-shift symmetry, and conserved total phase momentum are implemented | No spacetime covariance, tensor gauge symmetry, or physical unification is established |
| 7. Continuum-field refinement | Extend the action ansatz to a periodic one-dimensional scalar field and refine the spatial grid | Grid error decreases and energy drift remains bounded in the tested numerical run | A 1-D classical toy model is not a complete relativistic or empirically validated field theory |

The sequence approaches approximately 0.10406 for the specified toy model. This supports numerical convergence of that experiment's diagnostic, not the physical interpretation of the feedback law.

### 3.2 Experiment 5 representative run

For the recorded run with (\Delta\lambda=0.002), 2,000 steps, and (g=0.1):

- Control final clock coordinate: (-0.20225356673).
- Coupled final clock coordinate: (-0.169280242899).
- Final scalar-state distance: (0.00818203200525).
- Maximum state distance: (0.00904232538871).
- Coupled Hamiltonian drift: approximately (-1.28\times10^{-7}).

The coupled and uncoupled branches have different Hamiltonians because the coupling changes the model and its initial energy; their absolute energies should not be treated as directly interchangeable.

### 3.3 Experiment 6 symmetry and controlled limits

The global phase shift leaves the phase-difference potential invariant. The associated canonical charge (P_\theta) is conserved by the continuous equations and tested numerically. At (g=0), the clock decouples and the field subsystem recovers the existing Hamiltonian field model with matched initial data. Scalar-only and phase-only restrictions recover structural forms of familiar finite graph models. These are consistency checks and reductions, not derivations of continuum physics.

### 3.4 Experiment 7 refinement results

The local numerical refinement run used final parameter time (t=0.5), (L=2\pi), (c=1), (m=0.4), (I=1), κ=0.8, and (g=0.05). The (N=256) solution was the reference.

The RMS field difference and clock-coordinate difference decrease as the grid is refined. The absolute energy drift also decreases over these runs. It is not a proof of convergence for all initial data, parameters, or boundary conditions.

The (N=256) reference endpoint in this run had clock coordinate approximately (0.09128560345) and total energy approximately (0.32283105408).

## 5. Conclusions

The experiments support five limited conclusions:

1. Field-derived activity can be defined as a candidate relational clock observable.
2. A positive state-dependent time reparameterization changes the clock label but preserves the state-space trajectory when the chain rule is respected.
3. A separately postulated clock-to-field feedback term changes the dynamics and has a numerically convergent effect in the tested finite model.
4. An autonomous clock coordinate can be coupled to finite-dimensional field states through a shared action while retaining a conserved Hamiltonian up to integration error.
5. A simple periodic scalar continuum extension can be numerically refined with decreasing field error and decreasing energy drift in the tested configuration.

The evidence does **not** yet demonstrate that time is a fundamental field, that OTFT unifies gravity and quantum mechanics, that the proposed clock interaction exists physically, or that the model matches observational data. The term “continuum experiment” refers to a spatially discretized PDE ansatz and its grid-refinement study, not a completed continuum limit theorem.

## 6. Limitations and open problems

- **Dimensional calibration:** most finite-dimensional prototypes use dimensionless variables and parameters. Units and reference scales must be specified before comparing coefficients with nature.
- **Covariance:** the present clock is a global mechanical coordinate, not a Lorentz-covariant spacetime field. A relativistic extension must define the spacetime metric, field representations, causal structure, and invariant action.
- **Geometry:** tensor-array components do not yet transform as geometric tensors; index structure, connection, and gauge behavior are unspecified.
- **Continuum limit:** Experiment 7 is one-dimensional, periodic, classical, and scalar. Higher-dimensional convergence, local clock fields, boundary-condition dependence, and well-posedness remain open.
- **Quantum theory:** no quantization, operator algebra, renormalization, or comparison with quantum field theory has been supplied.
- **Empirical predictions:** no independent observable, parameter estimate, likelihood, or comparison with astronomical, laboratory, or precision-clock data has yet been established.
- **Model selection:** bounded coupling observables and feedback laws are chosen ansätze. Alternatives must be compared rather than treating one convenient form as uniquely implied.

## 7. Recommended next research milestones

1. **Independent numerical verification:** obtain a fresh CI run for Experiment 7 across supported Python versions and inspect all test and example logs.
2. **Local clock-field extension:** replace the single global clock coordinate with a spatial clock field (T(x,t)), beginning with a controlled nonrelativistic model and explicit boundary conditions.
3. **Covariant action study:** determine whether a consistent Lorentz-invariant action can be written, with a clear metric signature, field dimensions, and symmetry structure. Do not assume this succeeds.
4. **Dimensional analysis:** assign units and reference scales to all fields, couplings, and observables.
5. **Recovery tests:** derive and numerically verify limits corresponding to established scalar-field and oscillator models.
6. **Falsifiable predictions:** identify a measurable deviation from established theory, estimate its scale, and define a test that could rule out the model.
